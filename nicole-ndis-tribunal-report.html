This evidence categorically demonstrates that Nicole requires active 1:1 support around the clock, with particular emphasis on the overnight period where life-threatening events regularly occur.

3. Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) — Seizure Evidence

Nicole's Functional Neurological Disorder is the primary driver of her intensive support needs. FND causes non-epileptic seizures that are functionally identical in presentation to epileptic seizures — involving loss of consciousness, shaking, tremors, jerking, and post-ictal confusion. These episodes are unpredictable, occur at any time of day or night, and carry significant risk of injury from falls, aspiration, and secondary complications due to her concurrent Type 1 Diabetes. Support staff report that Nicole experiences 2–3 seizure episodes per day on average, making this a constant and pervasive feature of her daily life that requires continuous vigilance from support workers.

3.3 Physical Danger and Risk of Serious Harm

Nicole's FND-related seizures pose an immediate and ongoing risk of serious physical harm. Unlike conditions where episodes can be predicted or pre-empted, Nicole's seizures strike without warning during everyday activities — standing in the kitchen, sitting in her wheelchair, showering, walking to the toilet, or simply reaching for an item. The unpredictable nature of these episodes means that Nicole is at constant risk of:

Documented Injury Risks from Seizure Episodes:
  • Falling from standing or seated position: Nicole has been found unconscious on the floor on multiple occasions (17 Apr, 21 Apr, 22 Apr, 2 May, 4 May). On 17 April, she collapsed while reaching for her handbag. On 22 April, she lost her balance walking to the toilet and fell. On 4 May, she seized while sitting on the floor next to her wheelchair. Each of these events carries risk of head injury, fractures, and concussion.
  • Falling in the bathroom (wet surfaces): Seizures have occurred during showering on 24 April and 9 April. The combination of wet, slippery surfaces and loss of consciousness creates an extreme risk of head trauma, drowning, or spinal injury. On 20 April, Nicole became unconscious in the bathroom while sitting on a chair washing her hands.
  • Falling from wheelchair: On 18 April, Nicole seized while sitting in her wheelchair in the kitchen. On 1 May, she seized in her wheelchair returning from the kitchen, dropping items. On 5 May, she seized in the kitchen while writing on a whiteboard and had to be manually transferred via wheelchair back to her room. Without immediate staff intervention, she could have fallen from the wheelchair onto hard flooring.
  • Choking and aspiration: Seizures cause loss of consciousness with uncontrolled muscle movements. If Nicole is eating or drinking when a seizure occurs — which is possible at any time — there is a significant risk of choking or aspiration. Staff must be present to position her safely and monitor her airway.
  • Injury from objects in the environment: During seizures, Nicole experiences violent shaking, tremors, and jerking. Staff have repeatedly documented needing to clear surrounding objects for safety, remove wedges, and ensure nothing is within striking distance. Without this intervention, Nicole could strike her head, limbs, or body against furniture, door frames, or hard surfaces.
  • Prolonged seizures without intervention: The longest documented episode lasted 24 minutes (30 April). On 9 April, seizures were on and off for approximately 1 hour. Prolonged seizure activity without medical monitoring and intervention increases the risk of oxygen deprivation, brain injury, and cardiac complications.
  • Post-seizure vulnerability: After seizures, Nicole experiences confusion, extreme fatigue, dizziness, and impaired mobility. She requires physical assistance to return to bed and cannot be left alone as she may attempt to stand and fall again. Post-seizure BGL drops further compound the danger — on 18 April, her BGL crashed to 4.2 mmol/L immediately after a seizure, creating a dual emergency.

With an average of 2–3 seizures per day, Nicole faces these dangers not as rare events but as a constant daily reality. The evidence is unambiguous: Nicole cannot be left unattended at any point. A seizure can occur while she is standing, sitting, showering, eating, using the toilet, or lying in bed. Each episode requires immediate physical intervention — supporting her head, clearing the environment, monitoring breathing, timing the episode, and providing post-seizure care. Without a support worker physically present and alert, any one of these episodes could result in traumatic brain injury, bone fractures, aspiration, or death. The sheer frequency of episodes — multiple times daily — means the risk is not theoretical but an ongoing, ever-present danger that demands continuous 1:1 active support.

4. Overnight Active Support Requirement

Nicole's support needs do not diminish at night. The convergence of her FND-related seizures, Type 1 Diabetes requiring continuous BGL monitoring, and her disrupted sleep pattern (often awake until 2–5 AM) means that active overnight support is a clinical necessity, not a lifestyle preference.

4.1 Overnight Activity Pattern

Analysis of 59 days of progress notes reveals the following overnight patterns:

Evidence of Active Night Support Needs:
  • BGL interventions between 10 PM – 6 AM documented on the vast majority of overnight shifts — including hypoglycaemic events requiring immediate food/juice provision at 1:30 AM, 2:30 AM, 3:29 AM, 4:15 AM, 4:17 AM, 4:25 AM, etc.
  • Medication administration overnight — Nicole regularly requires PRN medications at midnight, 1 AM, 2:45 AM, 3 AM, and 4 AM including DayVigo, Oxazepam, Panamax, Metoclopramide, Ondansetron, and Nausicalm.
  • Nicole frequently awake until 1–5 AM — documented on 22 April ("hasn't slept whole night"), 20 April ("awake whole night"), 21 April ("didn't sleep whole night"), 30 April ("wasn't settled still late 2AM"), 1 May ("did not sleep at all from last night").
  • Overnight seizures documented on 13 Mar (1:42 AM), 21 Apr (5:44 AM), 23 Apr (2:25 AM), and 2 May (12:25 AM) — requiring immediate physical intervention.
  • Bed bath assistance, showering, and personal care frequently occurring after 10 PM due to Nicole's disrupted circadian rhythm.

4.2 Why Sleepover Support is Inadequate

A passive sleepover model — where a support worker sleeps on-site and is available "if needed" — is fundamentally incompatible with Nicole's needs for the following evidenced reasons:

  1. BGL alarms require immediate response: Nicole's Dexcom CGM system alerts when BGL drops below safe levels. On multiple occasions (19 Apr at 4:17 AM, 3 May at 11:44 PM), the Dexcom system failed to alert or readings were inaccurate, requiring manual finger-prick checks by the support worker. A sleeping worker cannot perform proactive monitoring.
  2. Seizures during overnight hours are life-threatening: Nicole has been found unconscious on the floor at 5:44 AM (21 Apr) and 12:25 AM (2 May). A delayed response to a seizure — particularly combined with a hypoglycaemic event — could result in serious injury or death.
  3. Nicole is actively awake and requiring support: Progress notes consistently document Nicole requesting food, medication, tea, emotional support, and assistance with toileting throughout the night. This is not occasional — it is the established pattern across 59 days of documented care.
  4. Mental health crises occur at night: On 3 May at 11 PM, Nicole was found on the floor with self-inflicted minor cuts on her arm. On 27 April, Nicole expressed passive suicidal thoughts during a bed bath at 11:30 PM. These incidents require an awake, attentive support worker.

  • 19 April, 4:17 AM: BGL was low but support worker "didn't hear any notification on phone." Nicole had to press the call bell at 4:57 AM to alert staff.

These documented technology failures further underscore the need for active human monitoring — the CGM system cannot be relied upon as the sole safety net.
